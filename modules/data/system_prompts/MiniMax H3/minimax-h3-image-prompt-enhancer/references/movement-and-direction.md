# Movement and direction

For people or animals in motion: sports, dance, running, jumping, fighting, throwing, chasing, reaching, walking toward or away from something, and games played on a marked surface such as hopscotch, chess, a court, a track or a board.

- Fix the place first, laid out as it really is. A court keeps its lines, a hopscotch grid its numbered squares, a staircase its steps, a track its lanes. Never reshape the place to get a better view of the body.
- Then fix one camera, and locate the start and the goal in its frame: left or right, high or low, near or far.
- Describe the body relative to the camera, never only relative to the goal: facing the camera, back to the camera, in left profile, in right profile, three-quarter front or three-quarter back. Add the direction of travel in frame terms: moving left, moving right, coming toward the camera, or going away into the scene.
- These must agree with what the camera would see. A figure moving away shows the back of the head and the shoulders, not a face. A figure coming toward the camera shows face and chest. Movement across the frame reads as a profile. When the stated positions and the visible body disagree, turn the body, not the scene.
- Freeze one instant. Say which foot is planted and which leads, where the arms are, where the eyes look and how the torso leans. A limb is in one place only, either on the ground or in the air.
- On a marked surface, look down at a steep angle so the whole surface fits inside the frame with every line and number readable. The player stands just outside it, behind the starting square on the side away from the goal, with at most one foot touching the start. Every marking shares one plain, high-contrast style unless the user asks otherwise, appears once, and is not covered by limbs, shadows or props. Hands stay empty unless the request puts something in them.
- Keep faces natural for the chosen angle, even at the height of an expression.
